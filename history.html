A Journey Through Time

From Greek refugees to Roman municipium — the story of a city that survived for more than a thousand years.

540 BC

The Foundation of Elea

Greek people from Phocaea (modern-day Turkey) escaped the Persian invasion. They fought a great naval battle near Alalia (Corsica) against the Etruscans and Carthaginians. After the battle, they moved south and founded the city of Elea on the coast of Campania. The land was purchased from the local Oenotrian people.

5th Century BC

The Golden Age

Elea grew quickly. It became an important centre for trade in the Mediterranean. The philosopher Parmenides created the Eleatic School and gave the city a constitution. The city was stable and well-organised. The city walls were extended, and new neighbourhoods were built. During this period, Elea was one of the most important Greek cities in southern Italy.

4th Century BC

Porta Rosa and Urban Development

The city underwent a major urban redevelopment. Porta Rosa — a remarkable arched bridge — was built to connect the northern and southern parts of the city. The public baths, the sanctuary of Asclepius, and the large Temple of Athena were also constructed during this period.

273 BC

Alliance with Rome

During the Punic Wars against Carthage, Elea became an ally of Rome. The city provided ships and a safe harbour. In exchange, the Romans gave Elea political and economic autonomy. The city also kept the right to mint its own coins.

88 BC

Roman Municipium — The Name Changes to Velia

Elea officially became a Roman municipium and changed its name to Velia. However, the city remained strongly Greek in culture and language. Many famous Romans visited Velia, including the statesman Cicero, the general Lucius Aemilius Paullus, and the poet Horace, who came here for leisure and health reasons. The city was also known for its school of medicine.

1st – 5th Century AD

Decline and Abandonment

Over the centuries, the city faced serious environmental problems. Soil erosion and flooding became common. The harbours silted up, and the coastline changed. After the fall of the Western Roman Empire, the city was slowly abandoned. By the 6th century AD, Velia was completely deserted. The ruins were buried under mud and earth.

1927 – Today

Rediscovery and Excavations

In the 19th century, archaeologists started to suspect that an ancient city was buried near Ascea. The first systematic excavations began in 1927 under Amedeo Maiuri. In the 1960s, Mario Napoli discovered Porta Rosa and the Archaic quarter. In 2022, a team found an archaic temple of Athena with three bronze helmets from the Battle of Alalia. In 2026, the exhibition "Exiles and Victors: The Helmets of Velia" opened to the public.

A Recent Discovery: The Helmets of Velia

In 2022, archaeologists discovered an Archaic temple dedicated to Athena on the Acropolis of Velia. Inside the temple, they found a collection of weapons and three bronze helmets — two Greek and one Etruscan.

This discovery is very important because it is the first physical evidence of the Battle of Alalia (540 BC). The helmets were offered to the goddess Athena as a sign of gratitude for the victory. The exhibition "Exiles and Victors: The Helmets of Velia" opened in May 2026.
